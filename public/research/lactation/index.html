Postpartum acyclovir in serum and milk

In a five-person study after oral valacyclovir, the median observed acyclovir peak was at 1 hour in maternal serum and 4 hours in breast milk. A constant milk-to-serum multiplier cannot shift the peak. This research view compares that assumption with the published evidence; it does not generate individual forecasts.

First-dose evidence

Sheffield et al. collected paired samples after the first dose and on day 5. The FDA review, Table 1, reports the following summaries for the same five participants. Concentrations in µg/mL have the same numerical values in mg/L.

Median (range), first oral valacyclovir dose in the study
EndpointMaternal serumBreast milk
Time of peak (h)1.0 (1.0–2.0)4.0 (2.0–4.0)
Peak (mg/L)2.7 (2.0–3.4)4.2 (1.1–6.4)
AUC (mg·h/L)14.7 (8.7–17.7)26.9 (20.7–32.8)

The median of the individual milk-to-serum AUC ratios was 2.2 (range 1.4–2.6). This is not the ratio of the two group median AUCs. The three-hour difference between the median peak times is not a fitted transfer delay or a patient-level result.

What a fixed ratio misses

Instantaneous ratio: If milk concentration is a positive constant times serum concentration at every time point, scaling changes the peak height and AUC, but it cannot change when the peak occurs. It predicts a median milk peak at 1 hour from the reported serum peak times, compared with 4 hours in the reported milk summaries.

Candidate with delayed equilibration: Model milk concentration as moving gradually toward a scaled serum concentration. In a first-order model, dCmilk/dt = k × (R × Cserum − Cmilk), where R is the equilibrium ratio and k is a rate in h⁻¹. This can produce a later peak. Neither parameter was fitted here: peak summaries alone do not establish the rate, validate a milk concentration–time curve, or distinguish transport mechanisms.

What would test a delayed model?

Compare both models at actual paired draw times, rather than comparing a simulated continuous maximum with the largest observed sample. If the first-dose and day-5 profiles can be recovered, use the first dose for fitting and hold day 5 out to test later predictions in the same cohort.

Drake et al. tested 44 milk samples from a separate cohort at two weeks postpartum. Use their full-text Results for the concentration units (µg/mL). The samples can contextualize the concentration range, but the time since dose was unavailable and infant drug concentrations were not measured.

Scope: Maternal serum and milk only. This evidence does not estimate infant plasma or fetal exposure, validate a patient-specific model, or guide dosing or breastfeeding decisions. The core AyFlux simulator is unchanged.
